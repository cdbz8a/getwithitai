Pull every deadline out of a long document in minutes.

Two free tools that show the page they read from, and why I still check every date myself.

Welcome back. A funding guide can bury the one date that matters on page 38, and missing it can mean waiting another year for the money. Today's fix turns that guide into a one-page deadline table you can check in a few minutes. Meanwhile, Google's NotebookLM now goes by Gemini Notebook, and its free plan holds 50 documents in a single notebook.

The Fix is your one AI skill for the week, built to use the same day.

THE FIX

Turn a long document into a deadline table

Funding guides and contribution agreements scatter their dates everywhere, from the cover letter to a footnote near the back. The only sure way to catch them all is reading the whole thing twice, and nobody at the band office has a spare afternoon for that.

  1. Open Gemini Notebook or Copilot Chat and start a new notebook or chat.
  2. Upload the PDF or Word file. If it has member names or file numbers in it, sign in with your work account or take those pages out first.
  3. Paste the prompt below and run it.
  4. Click two or three of the page references and check each date against the document yourself.
  5. Copy the table into your calendar and set a reminder two weeks before each date.
Read the attached document.
Job: find every deadline and reporting date, and who has to act on it.
Who it's for: me, an office worker who can't miss a date.
Shape: a table with four columns: date, what's due, who does it, page or section. Sort it by date.
Background: if a date isn't stated outright, write "not stated" and quote the line it came from. Don't add any date that isn't in the document.

When it works: Gemini Notebook answers from the files you give it and shows the passage it used, so checking a date is one click. A free account gets up to 50 sources per notebook, according to Google's plans page, and each source can run to 500,000 words or 200MB, according to Google's FAQ.

Watch for: Dates written as "30 days after signing" or "end of the fiscal year" are the ones that get missed or turned into a calendar date the document never gave. That's why the prompt asks for "not stated" with the quoted line, and why step 4 happens every single time.

TOOL PICK

Two tools that read your files and show their work

1. Gemini Notebook: Google's research tool, called NotebookLM until it was renamed on 16 July, answers questions from documents you upload and suits anyone working through long reports. The Standard plan is free with a Gmail account, and on a personal account Google says your content isn't used to directly train its models unless you send thumbs up or down feedback, which human reviewers can see.

2. Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat: If your office already runs on Microsoft 365, Copilot Chat is automatically included with the subscription and can read the PDFs, PowerPoint files and spreadsheets you upload. Signed in with a work account, Microsoft says your prompts and uploaded files aren't used to train its models, and the uploads are stored in your own OneDrive for Business, where you can delete them.

MY TAKE

The table is a head start, and the dates are still yours

I'd run this on every funding guide that lands on my desk, but I wouldn't put a single date in the calendar until I'd clicked through to the page it came from. A missed reporting date costs a community real money, and "the AI missed it" won't bring that money back. The habit worth building is splitting the job in two: the tool does the first read of the whole document, and you do the second read on the dates only. That second read takes about ten minutes. It's still a lot faster than reading 50 pages twice, and you'll trust the calendar when it reminds you.
