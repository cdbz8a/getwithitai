Lesson 1 of 7 · 4 min

What you're actually talking to

It's a program that predicts words, and it's very good at it.

When people talk about AI these days, they usually mean a chat app like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Microsoft Copilot. You open it in a web browser or on your phone, type the way you'd normally talk, and it types back.

It learned from a huge amount of human writing, and it works by predicting which words should come next. That's why it's so good at drafting a letter, explaining a confusing form or summarizing ten pages of notes. It's also why it can give you a wrong date or a made-up rule in the same confident voice it uses when it's right.

The rule for the whole course: whatever AI gives you is a first draft, and you're the editor. You're responsible for the facts and the judgment calls, and it saves you the time.

Try it: ask it about itself
You Explain what you are and how you come up with your answers, in plain language, in five sentences. Then tell me the two most common kinds of mistakes you make and how I can catch them.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

Look at the second part of the answer. The app will tell you itself that it makes mistakes. Keep that in mind when you get to lesson 5.

Lesson 2 of 7 · 4 min

Open one app and give it a real job

Pick one app for the week and give it something off your to-do list.

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot all have free versions, and for everyday writing they work about the same. Pick one and use it all week so you get comfortable with it. If your workplace already gives you Copilot through Microsoft 365, start there.

Then give it a real job, something you'd have spent ten minutes on anyway. A reply you've been putting off works well, and so do meeting notes or a letter you'd like explained in plain English.

  • "Turn these meeting notes into a list of action items with who is doing what: [paste notes]"
  • "Explain this letter in plain English and tell me what I need to do and by when: [paste it]"

Read what it gives you before you send anything. For now the point is just to see that you've got a starting point on the screen.

Try it: a real reply
You Write a short, friendly reply to a coworker who asked me to cover a meeting on Friday. I can't make Friday, but I can cover Monday morning and I'm happy to read their notes ahead of time.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

That took about fifteen seconds. Think about how many emails you've got sitting in your inbox that you could handle the same way.

Next: The four-part prompt
Lesson 3 of 7 · 4 min

The four-part prompt

Tell it the job, who it's for, the shape you want, and the background.

A vague request gets you a generic answer, and a clear one usually gets you something close to usable on the first try. You only need four things, and they fit in a couple of sentences:

  • The job: write a notice, reply to this email, summarize, explain, make a list
  • Who it's for: community members, your manager, a funder, a customer, your kid's teacher
  • The shape: under 100 words, five bullet points, one page, warm and plain
  • The background: the facts, dates and notes it needs, pasted right in

If there's something you know you won't like, say so at the end, something like "keep it out of government-form language." One line like that saves you a round of edits.

Try it: the four parts filled in
You Write a notice for community members about the office closing early this Friday for a staff meeting. It's for families who will see it on Facebook and on the front door. Keep it under 80 words, warm and clear, and keep it out of stiff government-form language. Background: the office closes at noon Friday and reopens Monday at 8 a.m. The emergency line stays open all weekend.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

Now try one of your own jobs. Fill in the four parts and paste in your notes. Most of what you'll ever ask AI to do fits that pattern.

Next: Steer the draft
Lesson 4 of 7 · 4 min

Steer the draft

Keep talking to it and tell it exactly what to change.

The first answer usually needs some work, and that's normal. You're in a conversation, so tell it what to fix and it'll rewrite the whole thing in a few seconds:

  • "Make it half as long."
  • "Make it friendlier. This sounds stiff."
  • "Drop the second paragraph and get to the point sooner."
  • "That's off. What I meant was [explain]. Try again."

Two or three rounds of that usually gets you something you'd put your name on. A lot of people give up after one weak answer, which is a shame, because this is the easy part.

Here's a trick worth learning early. When you're not sure what you need, have it ask you questions first. You answer each one in a few words, and the draft that comes back fits your situation a lot better.

Try it: let it interview you
You I need to write a short report for my manager about a project that's running behind. Before you write anything, ask me up to five questions about what I need. Wait for my answers, then write the report.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

Answer its questions in a few words each, then ask it to change one thing in the draft. Going back and forth like that is most of the skill.

Next: Check what matters
Lesson 5 of 7 · 4 min

Check what matters

It sounds just as sure of itself when it's wrong.

Sometimes AI gets something flat wrong, like a date, a dollar figure, a policy, a quote or someone's job title. It says it in the same smooth voice it uses when it's right. These mistakes are called hallucinations, and they catch a lot of new users.

The habit that protects you is simple. Check anything that would embarrass you if it turned out to be wrong. That means names, numbers, dates, laws and health claims, plus anything going to your manager, council, a funder or the public. A quick look at the original document or a ten-second search usually does it.

The best way to see this for yourself is to test it on something you know really well.

Try it: test it on something you know
You Tell me five facts about [your town or community]: when it was founded, roughly how many people live there, and three things it's known for. For each fact, tell me how sure you are.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

Compare its answer with what you know. Most people find at least one thing that's off, and it usually sounds just as sure about the wrong fact as the right ones. Let AI help with the wording and keep the facts in your own hands.

Next: Keep private details out
Lesson 6 of 7 · 4 min

Keep private details out

If you wouldn't write it on a postcard, keep it out of a public AI app.

Free public AI tools are the wrong place for full names with addresses, phone numbers, member or account numbers, health details, or anything confidential from work. Once you paste something in, you can't take it back. If your workplace has its own AI rules or an approved tool, follow those first.

The workaround takes about thirty seconds:

  • Swap the real details for placeholders like "Member A," "my coworker," "Unit 3" or "[date]"
  • Get your draft from the AI
  • Paste it into your own email or document and put the real details back in there

You still get the speed, and the private details stay on your own computer. If you start doing this now, it'll be automatic in a week.

Try it: write with placeholders
You Write a respectful, clear letter to Member A about paperwork for their file that is now overdue. Let them know we want to help, list what they need to bring in, and give them until [date] to drop it off at [office]. Keep it under 150 words.
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

You got a finished letter without typing in a single real name or number. That's all there is to it.

Next: Make it sound like you
Lesson 7 of 7 · 4 min

Make it sound like you

Give it a sample of your writing, then save the prompts that work.

People can usually tell when something was written by AI. It loves words like "delve," "tapestry," "robust," "seamless" and "pivotal." It opens with lines like "In today's fast-paced world," puts everything in groups of three, and uses a lot of long dashes. Once people notice that, they trust the message less, and they trust the person who sent it less too.

The fix is to show it how you write. Paste in two or three emails you wrote yourself, ask it to describe your style, and then have it rewrite drafts to match. Tell it which words to stay away from. After a while you'll spot the giveaway phrases on your own and cut them in a few seconds.

One last step. Open the notes app on your phone and start a note called "My prompts." Save the prompts from these lessons that worked best for you. That note is your prompt library, and you'll add to it every time something works.

Try it: teach it your voice
You Here are two emails I wrote myself: [paste them]. Describe my writing style in five short bullet points. Then rewrite the draft below so it sounds like me. Use plain everyday words, keep my sentence lengths, and leave out the words delve, robust, seamless and pivotal. Draft: [paste the draft]
Open in: ChatGPT Claude Gemini Copilot

Read the rewrite out loud. If it sounds like something you'd actually say, save that prompt first in your library.
